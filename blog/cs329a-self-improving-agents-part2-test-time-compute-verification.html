Self-Improving AI Agents, Part 2: Test-Time Compute & Verification

Part 1 introduced the course's central claim: once a model can generate many candidate answers, the bottleneck shifts from generation to verification. This part covers the two lectures that unpack each half of that claim — Lecture 2 on getting more (and better) candidates out of a fixed model at inference time, and Lecture 3 on how to actually tell which candidate is right.

Recap: Why These Two Lectures Come First

Part 1 covered the course's four-phase framing — scale, post-training, test-time compute, agentic loops — and its organizing thesis that verification, not generation, sets the ceiling on how much a model benefits from generating more candidate answers. Lectures 2 and 3 are where that thesis gets made concrete: Lecture 2 is entirely about generation (how much better can you do by sampling more, searching more, spending more compute per query), and Lecture 3 is entirely about verification (once you have many candidates, how do you actually pick the right one). Read together, they form a single argument: more candidates only help if you can tell them apart, and telling them apart turns out to be the harder problem.

Test-Time Compute Scaling

The course's Lecture 2, per its assigned readings (Brown et al. 2024, Snell et al. 2024, Saad-Falcon et al. 2024, and Schaeffer et al. 2025 — all listed directly in the course-note mirrors), covers what happens when you hold a model's weights fixed and instead spend more compute at inference time.

Repeated sampling and coverage curvesLecture 2
The core empirical pattern the lecture builds from, per the course-note mirrors: as you let a model attempt a problem more times (sampling many independent candidate solutions), the chance that at least one attempt is correct climbs — often dramatically. One example cited in the notes: DeepSeek-Coder-V2's success rate on SWE-bench Lite reportedly rose from roughly 15.9% at a single attempt to around 56% at 250 sampled attempts. That's a "coverage" curve, not an accuracy curve — it tells you the ceiling of what's reachable among your samples, not how often you'd actually pick the right one without a good way to select it. This gap between "the right answer is somewhere in there" and "you found it" is what makes the next lecture necessary.
📖 Cited in course notes: Brown et al. 2024, Snell et al. 2024, Saad-Falcon et al. 2024, Schaeffer et al. 2025
Parallel vs. sequential test-time strategiesLecture 2
The lecture reportedly distinguishes two broad ways to spend extra inference compute: parallel strategies (best-of-n — generate many independent candidates and pick the best one) and sequential strategies (revision — generate one answer, then critique and revise it, repeatedly). Which one is more compute-efficient depends on the problem's difficulty: per the course notes, harder problems tend to benefit more from sequential revision, while easier problems get diminishing returns from either past a certain point — motivating a "compute-optimal allocation" idea, where how much test-time compute you spend (and how you spend it) should depend on the difficulty of the specific problem in front of you, not be fixed in advance.
📖 Reported in course notes, not independently verified

Archon: Treating Agent Design as Search

ArchonLecture 2
Named in the course notes as an architecture-search framework for inference-time systems: rather than a person hand-designing how a pipeline samples, verifies, and selects among candidates, Archon is described as searching over that design space itself — treating choices like "how many samples," "which verification method," and "how to combine sub-components" as something to optimize automatically rather than guess at. This is a genuinely RSI-adjacent idea even though it lives inside a test-time-compute lecture rather than a self-modifying-code lecture: it's automated design of the inference pipeline, one level up from automated generation of the answer itself.
📖 Named in course notes; not independently verified against a primary Archon paper/repo

From Outcome Rewards to Process Rewards

Lecture 3 covers verification directly, tracing what the course notes describe as a progression in how reward/verification signals for reasoning have evolved:

1
Outcome Reward Models (ORMs). Judge only the final answer — right or wrong — with no credit for the reasoning that got there. Cobbe et al. 2021 (the GSM8K math-word-problem benchmark paper) is cited as the reference point for this era: a model's chain of reasoning could be entirely wrong and still get full credit if it stumbled onto the right final number, or entirely sound and get zero credit for a single arithmetic slip at the end.
2
Process Reward Models (PRMs). Score each step of a reasoning chain individually, not just the final answer. Lightman et al. 2023, "Let's Verify Step by Step," is cited as the key paper here, introducing the PRM800K dataset of step-level human annotations used to train this kind of verifier. The course notes frame this as a direct response to ORMs rewarding correct answers reached via unsound reasoning — a PRM can catch a bad step even when the final answer happens to be right.
3
Math-Shepherd and automated process supervision. Wang et al. 2023 is cited for a method to generate step-level supervision automatically, rather than requiring expensive human step-by-step annotation the way PRM800K did — using the model's own rollouts from each step to estimate whether that step was actually on a path to the correct answer.
Why this progression matters for self-improvement specifically: a verifier that only judges final answers can't tell you where a multi-step agent went wrong, which means it can't generate a useful training or correction signal for the intermediate steps — only for whether to keep or discard the whole attempt. A step-level verifier can. That's the difference between a system that can only get better at picking good full attempts, and one that can actually learn from and correct its own partial mistakes — a prerequisite for the multi-step, tool-using agents covered in Part 3 of this series.

Weaver: Ensembling Weak Verifiers

WeaverSaad-Falcon et al., NeurIPS 2025
The most recent verification paper cited in Lecture 3's notes. Rather than relying on one large, expensive, carefully-trained verifier, Weaver is described as fusing many weaker, cheaper verification signals together — using an EM-style (expectation-maximization) approach to estimate each weak verifier's reliability and combine them into a single stronger judgment. The course notes report a headline accuracy figure around 87.7%, though the exact benchmark and comparison baseline for that number were not independently confirmed here. Conceptually, this closes the loop with Lecture 2's "cheap-and-many vs. expensive-and-few" theme: Weaver applies that same idea to verifiers themselves, not just to answer candidates.
📖 Reported in course notes; exact figures and benchmark not independently verified

Why These Two Lectures Are Really One Idea

The pattern that repeats at every level: generate cheaply and verify carefully — then automate the verification too, once it becomes the bottleneck. Lecture 2 applies this to answers (sample many, pick the best). Lecture 3 applies it to the verifier itself (start with one expensive signal, move to step-level signals, then ensemble many cheap signals via Weaver). Archon applies it one level higher still, to the design of the whole pipeline. If you're looking for the single idea to carry into Part 3 (feedback loops and multi-step planning), it's this: every agentic system in the rest of this course is built out of exactly this generate-then-verify unit, repeated and nested.

⚠️ Confidence Notes and Gaps

Lecture 2's content is corroborated across two independent course-note mirrors and is reported here with reasonable confidence, though specific figures (the 15.9%→56% coverage curve, exact compute-optimal allocation claims) are as-reported by those mirrors, not verified against Brown et al. 2024 or Snell et al. 2024 directly. Lecture 3's content rests more on a single detailed mirror; Weaver's 87.7% figure and the precise mechanics of Archon's search process specifically were not cross-checked against a second source or the original papers. If you're citing specific numbers from this part in your own work, read Lightman et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2023, and Saad-Falcon et al.'s Weaver paper directly rather than relying on this summary.
