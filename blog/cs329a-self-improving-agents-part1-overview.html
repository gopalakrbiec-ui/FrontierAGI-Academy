Self-Improving AI Agents, Part 1: Course Overview

A lecture-by-lecture guide to Stanford's CS329A, "Self-Improving AI Agents" — nine lectures on the mechanics that let today's AI systems get better at reasoning, tool use, and long-horizon tasks without a human rewriting their weights by hand. This opening part covers what the course is, who teaches it, and the four-phase framing its first lecture uses to organize everything that follows.

What the Course Is

CS329A, "Self-Improving AI Agents," is a Stanford course taught by Aakanksha Chowdhery (Reflection AI) and Azalia Mirhoseini (Stanford), delivered as a nine-lecture series running roughly 10 hours and 47 minutes in total, published as a public YouTube playlist. No public syllabus, formal reading list, or prerequisite list was found for the course as a whole during research for this guide — the one exception is Lecture 2, whose course-note mirrors list four assigned papers directly (Brown et al. 2024, Snell et al. 2024, Saad-Falcon et al. 2024, and Schaeffer et al. 2025, all concerning test-time compute scaling, covered in Part 2 of this series).

The course's subject is not recursive self-improvement in the architectural sense — it isn't about agents rewriting their own source code the way a Gödel Machine or Darwin Gödel Machine does. It's about the mechanisms that let a fixed model get more out of itself: how much better an LLM's answers get if you let it think longer, sample more, verify its own work, and act inside a feedback loop with tools — and how those same mechanisms compose into agents capable of long-horizon, multi-step tasks. That's a different, complementary branch of "self-improvement" from the site's existing RSI coverage, which is why this is being published as its own series rather than folded into that one.

The Four-Phase Framing

The opening lecture, per the course-note mirrors, organizes the last several years of AI progress into four phases — a structure the rest of the course's lectures each slot into:

1
Scale. Scaling laws and emergent abilities — the era where making models bigger and training on more data reliably produced better performance, and where qualitatively new capabilities appeared at certain scale thresholds.
2
Post-training. RLHF and the ChatGPT-era shift to instruction tuning plus reward models — teaching an already-trained model to actually follow instructions and align its outputs with what a reward signal (often derived from human preference data) says is good.
3
Test-time compute. "Let it think longer" — instead of only improving the model itself, spend more compute at inference time: sample many candidate answers, search over reasoning paths, verify and select among them. This is where Part 2 of this series picks up.
4
Agentic loops. Goal → plan → act → observe → self-correct — wrapping a model in a loop where it takes actions in an environment, observes the results, and adjusts, rather than producing one answer and stopping. This is where the course's later lectures (and Parts 3–5 of this series) live.

Read as a whole, the four phases describe a shift in where "improvement" happens: from the model's weights (phases 1–2) to how the model is used at inference and inside a loop (phases 3–4). That shift — improvement moving outward from training into inference and agent scaffolding — is arguably the single idea the rest of the course spends its remaining eight lectures unpacking.

The Core Thesis: Verification, Not Generation, Sets the Ceiling

The course's stated organizing claim, per the opening-lecture notes: once a model can generate many candidate solutions — through sampling, search, or multi-step reasoning — the bottleneck on how good the final answer gets is no longer generation quality. It's whether you can reliably tell which candidate is actually correct. A system that generates brilliant answers alongside plausible-looking wrong ones, with no way to tell them apart, gets no benefit from generating more of them.

This single claim previews most of the course's structure: Lecture 2 is about generating more candidates cheaply (test-time compute scaling); Lecture 3 is about telling candidates apart reliably (robust verification, reward models); Lectures 4–5 are about doing this inside a real feedback loop with tools and multi-step plans, where each step needs its own verification; and Lecture 6 pushes verification-driven selection back into training itself, via reinforcement learning methods that use exactly this kind of signal to update the model's weights. Parts 2 through 5 of this series follow that same order.

What This Series Covers

This is planned as a six-part guide, grouping the course's nine lectures into thematically coherent parts rather than a strict one-lecture-per-article structure, since several lectures are short and closely related:

1
Part 1 — Course Overview (this piece). The four-phase framing and the verification thesis.
2
Part 2 — Test-Time Compute & Verification. Lecture 2's sampling/coverage curves and the Archon architecture-search framework, plus Lecture 3's progression from outcome reward models to process reward models to ensemble verification (Math-Shepherd, Weaver).
3
Part 3 — Feedback, Tools & Planning. Lecture 4's ReAct, RLEF, and Constitutional AI/RLAIF, plus Lecture 5's multi-step reasoning and search methods (Search-in-the-Chain, SPRINT, SWiRL).
4
Part 4 — Scaling RL for Reasoning. Lecture 6's train-time scaling methods — STaR, GRPO, DAPO — and how verification-driven selection gets folded back into the training loop itself.
5
Part 5 — Self-Improving & Deep Research Agents. Lecture 7's AlphaCode/AlphaCode 2 case study and the agentic RAG pattern behind products like deep-research assistants.
6
Part 6 — Evaluation & What's Next. Lecture 8's long-horizon evaluation benchmarks and Lecture 9's six forward-looking research directions.

Each part will carry the same sourcing caveat as this one, name every technique and paper explicitly, and flag anything that rests on a single unverified mirror rather than being cross-checked.

⚠️ Confidence Notes and Gaps

This overview is built primarily from one detailed mirror (a GitHub repository containing English transcripts and per-lecture summaries) with partial cross-checking against a second mirror that has real notes for four of the nine lectures, and a third mirror that confirms the lecture count, order, and titles via structured notes but was not read for detailed content. No syllabus, formal reading list, or prerequisites were found for the course as a whole. The four-phase framing and the "verification, not generation" thesis are both reported consistently across the sources checked, which is why they're presented with reasonable confidence here — but neither was verified against the official course page or the video itself.
